## 2. Per-item key principles

### 2.1 Introduction to UX

**Definition of UX** (Norman & Nielsen, 1998). https://www.nngroup.com/articles/definition-user-experience/
- UX is everything an end user experiences with a company, its services and its products, not only the screen.
- The first goal is meeting the customer's actual need with no fuss. Simplicity and elegance come next.
- Feature checklists and "ask customers what they want" are not UX.
- UI is a subset of UX. A great search UI over a catalog that lacks the right content is still a bad experience.
- Usability is a quality attribute of the UI (learnable, efficient, pleasant). UX is the broader whole.
- Good UX needs engineering, marketing, graphic, industrial and interface design to work as one.

**Usability 101** (Nielsen, 2012, reviewed 2026). https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-101-introduction-to-usability/
- Usability has five components: learnability, efficiency, memorability, errors (rate, severity, recoverability) and satisfaction.
- Utility (does it do what's needed) plus usability (is it easy) together make usefulness. Neither suffices alone.
- Core method: representative users doing representative tasks, observed without help. About 5 users per round, many small rounds.
- Watch what users do rather than what they say. Focus groups are weak for usability.
- Test early and at every iteration. Late-found problems are structural and expensive.
- Benchmark claim: spend about 10% of the project budget on usability. NN/g says this roughly doubles quality metrics, which is an NN/g figure.

**UX vs. UI** (video, Gibbons, 2021). https://www.nngroup.com/videos/ux-vs-ui/
- Video only (title-level). The terms are related, often confused, and both matter.

**Don Norman on the term "UX"** (video, 2016). https://www.nngroup.com/videos/don-norman-term-ux/
- Video only. The page does not include Norman's answer.

**The Design of Everyday Things** (video, Norman, 2016). https://www.nngroup.com/videos/don-norman-design-everyday-things/
- Video only. It covers the book's origin.

**Principles of Human-Centered Design** (video, Norman, 2018). https://www.nngroup.com/videos/principles-human-centered-design-don-norman/
- HCD is a mindset, not a fixed process.
- Keep people at the center.
- Design the whole system, not just the interface.
- Solve the right problem, so problem-finding comes first.

**The Immutable Rules of UX** (video keynote, Nielsen, 2019). https://www.nngroup.com/videos/rules-ux/
- Video only. Its premise is that core UX principles have stayed stable for decades.

**Design Thinking 101** (Gibbons, 2016, reviewed 2026). https://www.nngroup.com/articles/design-thinking/
- Three macro-stages: understand, explore, materialize.
- Six phases: empathize, define, ideate, prototype, test, implement.
- Implementation is the most-neglected phase and is where impact happens.
- The phases are iterative and non-linear. Expect to loop back after testing.
- It is a scaffold, not a recipe, and scales from a feature to an organizational problem.

**Design Thinking 101** (video, 2019). https://www.nngroup.com/videos/design-thinking/
- Video only. Same content as the article.

**Jakob's Law** (video, Nielsen, 2020). https://www.nngroup.com/videos/jakobs-law-internet-user-experience/
- The law: users spend most of their time on other sites/products, so they expect yours to work like those. Stated in the heuristics article: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics/
- The video discusses the law's shortcomings, but the page does not say what they are.

**Mental Models** (Chan, 2024, from Nielsen 2010). https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mental-models/
- A mental model is what the user believes about how the system works. It drives their predictions and actions and can be wrong.
- Models are individual, built from prior products, and sticky. Users cling to familiar models.
- Designers know their system too well, so designer-obvious is not user-obvious.
- Fix a mismatch two ways: change the system to fit the model, or teach the model through labels, explanation and onboarding.
- Innovate on conventions only when the new way is clearly better.
- Repeated identical user errors signal a wrong mental model. Think-aloud testing reveals models.

**Interaction Cost** (Budiu, 2013, reviewed 2024). https://www.nngroup.com/articles/interaction-cost-definition/
- Interaction cost is the sum of mental and physical effort to reach a goal. NN/g treats it as a direct measure of usability.
- Cost components: reading, scrolling, searching, comprehending, precise pointing or tapping, typing, waiting/loads, attention switches, and memory load.
- Cost varies by user (motor, reading ability) and by context (device, network).
- Users weigh expected benefit against expected cost and pick the cheaper of comparable options.
- Reduce it with scannable text, large targets, key controls placed where expected, plain headings and short waits.

**Why Interaction Cost Matters** (video, Budiu, 2018). https://www.nngroup.com/videos/interaction-cost/
- Higher interaction cost means a harder interface. Summary only.

**Dualities of UX** (video keynote, Nielsen, 2020). https://www.nngroup.com/videos/dualities-user-experience/
- Many UX questions have two good but opposing answers. Both extremes have value, and the practitioner's job is to resolve the tension in context.

### 2.3 UX Planning and Research

**UX Without User Research Is Not UX** (Loranger, 2014). https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-without-user-research/
- Users judge usefulness and ease within seconds.
- UX is everyone's responsibility, not one department's.
- Deadline pressure and Agile setups without UX roles cause research to be skipped, and shipping untested work wastes effort.
- Valid research needs real target users (not colleagues), non-leading questions, and a method that fits the stage.
- Test sketches and wireframes before code, using the cheapest artifact that answers the question.
- Watching users struggle settles internal debates and persuades stakeholders.

**When to Use Which UX Research Methods** (Rohrer, 2022, reviewed 2026). https://www.nngroup.com/articles/which-ux-research-methods/
- Attitudinal (what people say) vs. behavioral (what they do). The two diverge, so lean on behavior for usability.
- Qualitative answers "why" and "how to fix". Quantitative answers "how many" and "how much".
- Context of use ranges from natural to scripted to none, trading realism against control.
- By stage: generative (new directions), formative (improve during build), summative (measure after launch).
- Pick the method for the question and the stage, and mix methods rather than defaulting to a favorite.

**When to Use Which UX Research Method** (video, Pernice, 2018). https://www.nngroup.com/videos/when-use-which-ux-research-method/
- Don't default to usability testing. The video gives five criteria for picking a method (the page does not list them).

**UX Research Cheat Sheet** (Farrell, 2017). https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-research-cheat-sheet/
- **Discover:** field studies, diary studies, user and stakeholder interviews, requirements and constraints gathering.
- **Explore:** personas, journey maps, task analysis, design review, competitive analysis, user stories, card sorting.
- **Test:** qualitative usability testing (core), benchmark tests (time on task, success rate, error rate), accessibility evaluation.
- **Listen:** surveys, analytics, search-log analysis, usability-bug review, FAQ review.
- If you can do only one thing to improve an existing system, run think-aloud testing. Analytics show what happened, not why.
- Front-load research effort but reserve budget for small studies later.

**UX Research Cheat Sheet** (video, Gibbons, 2020). https://www.nngroup.com/videos/ux-research-cheat-sheet/
- Same four goals: discover, explore, test, listen. Research belongs at any point in the cycle.

**User Interviews 101** (Rosala & Pernice, 2023, reviewed 2026). https://www.nngroup.com/articles/user-interviews/
- Use interviews for discovery: experiences, needs, motivations. They yield attitudes and reported behavior, never reactions to a design.
- Set concrete research questions. Vague goals give unactionable findings.
- Use a piloted guide with open questions and follow-ups, asking about specific past events.
- Build rapport, never interrupt, probe for "why", and never lead.
- Self-report suffers from recall gaps and social-desirability bias. Triangulate with behavioral methods.

**Personas** (Dykes, 2025). https://www.nngroup.com/articles/persona/
- A persona is a fictional character standing for a target user, and it must be grounded in research, not assumptions.
- Create personas early and as a team, so the team trusts them.
- Every detail must earn its place by affecting a design decision.
- A persona's name becomes shared shorthand that replaces each person's private idea of "the user".
- Later uses: partner briefs, expert reviews (which don't replace testing), recruitment screeners, analytics segments.

**Creating Personas Is Like Sorting Rocks** (video, Flaherty, 2018). https://www.nngroup.com/videos/creating-personas-sorting-rocks/
- Segmenting the data is the hardest step. Look for shared traits across attributes.

**UX Mapping Cheat Sheet** (Gibbons, 2017). https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-mapping-cheat-sheet/
- **Empathy map:** says/thinks/feels/does for one user type. Non-sequential.
- **Journey map:** one user's chronological path through one product, with emotions.
- **Experience map:** the same kind of path, but product-agnostic and general.
- **Service blueprint:** the provider-side backstage, showing employees, processes and systems behind each touchpoint.
- Decide before mapping: current or future state, assumption-based or research-based, low or high fidelity.
- The mapping process builds a shared team mental model, which is the main value.

**IA vs. Navigation** (Cardello, 2014). https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ia-vs-navigation/
- IA is the content and function inventory plus its relationships. Navigation is the set of UI components that expose it (global, local, utility, breadcrumbs, filters).
- IA informs UI. IA is documented in sheets and diagrams, not wireframes.
- Users never see IA, but bad IA creates friction everywhere.
- Pick navigation patterns for structure, not looks. An inverted-L handles about 4 levels.
- Draft a first-pass IA before wireframing.

**Prototype Fidelity** (Pernice, 2016). https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-prototype-hi-lo-fidelity/
- Fidelity has three independent axes: interactivity, visuals and content.
- Throwing away a prototype is far cheaper than reworking shipped code.
- Use high fidelity for workflows, detailed UI, legibility and realistic timing. Use low fidelity for fast early iteration and less participant pressure.
- Static prototypes need a human "computer", whose errors can corrupt mental models and trust. Pilot-test the prototype first.

**Prototypes vs. Wireframes** (video, Kendrick, 2019). https://www.nngroup.com/videos/prototypes-vs-wireframes-ux-projects/
- Video only. It covers how the two differ and which audiences each suits.

**User Testing: Why & How** (video, Nielsen, 2018). https://www.nngroup.com/videos/user-testing-jakob-nielsen/
- Testing is fast, cheap and persuasive, so there is no excuse to skip it.
- Recruit representative users and give them realistic tasks. Expect surprises.

**Usability Testing 101** (Moran, 2019, reviewed 2026). https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-testing-101/
- Three elements: facilitator, tasks, participant.
- The facilitator must not steer the participant. Task wording can prime or confuse.
- Use think-aloud to expose reasoning.
- Qualitative testing (most common, about 5 per user group) finds problems. Quantitative testing (success, time) benchmarks.
- Sessions can be moderated remote, unmoderated remote or in person. A discount study takes about 3 days of work.

**Usability Testing 101** (video, Moran, 2022). https://www.nngroup.com/videos/usability-testing-101/
- Video only. Same framing as the article.

**Why You Only Need 5 Users** (Nielsen, 2000). https://www.nngroup.com/articles/why-you-only-need-to-test-with-5-users/
- Problems found ≈ N·(1−(1−L)^n), with L ≈ 31% per user, so 5 users find about 85% of problems.
- Returns diminish quickly. Spend a 15-user budget as 3 rounds of 5, with fixes between rounds.
- Avoid n=1, because a single user may be unrepresentative.
- Caveats: with distinct user groups, test 3–4 per group. Quantitative studies need about 20 or more users, and card sorting about 15.

**Thematic Analysis** (video, Rosala, 2019). https://www.nngroup.com/videos/thematic-analysis-qualitative-user-research-data/
- Code raw qualitative data (transcripts, notes) into themes to produce actionable findings.

**Quantitative Methods Overview** (Moran, 2018). https://www.nngroup.com/articles/quantitative-user-research-methods/
- Quantitative research measures usability, compares designs and competitors, tests significance and supports ROI cases.
- General questions call for benchmarking, analytics or surveys. Specific questions call for A/B testing, card sorting, tree testing, desirability studies or eyetracking.
- Sample sizes: about 35 or more for benchmarking, about 30 or more for eyetracking.
- Never decide on small samples or raw means, and never stop A/B tests early.

### 2.4 User-Interface Design

**5 Principles of Visual Design** (Gordon, 2020, reviewed 2026). https://www.nngroup.com/articles/principles-visual-design/
- **Scale:** the most important element is the largest. Use about 3 sizes at most.
- **Visual hierarchy:** order attention via size, value, color, spacing and placement. Have small, medium and large tiers. If users can't tell where to look first, hierarchy has failed.
- **Balance:** distribute visual weight (size times prominence) around an axis, symmetrical, asymmetrical or radial, chosen for the mood you want.
- **Contrast:** signals difference in category or function. **Never lower text contrast to de-emphasize**, and verify with a contrast checker.
- **Gestalt:** similarity, continuation, closure, proximity, common region, figure/ground, symmetry. Proximity matters most. Put a label nearer its own field than its neighbor's.

**Design Principles 101** (video, Rosala, 2020). https://www.nngroup.com/videos/design-principles-101/
- Product design principles are value statements for making trade-offs. They keep multiple teams' decisions consistent.

**10 Usability Heuristics** (Nielsen 1994, wording updated 2020, reviewed 2024). https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics/
- The ten: system status visibility, match with the real world, user control and freedom, consistency and standards, error prevention, recognition over recall, flexibility and efficiency, aesthetic and minimalist design, error recognition and recovery, help and documentation. See section 3 for agent checks.
- They are broad rules of thumb for heuristic evaluation, not detailed guidelines.
- Origin: Molich & Nielsen 1990, refined in 1994 from a factor analysis of 249 problems. NN/g says the content has been unchanged since 1994.
- Slips (inattention) are prevented with constraints and good defaults. Mistakes (wrong mental model) are prevented by reducing memory load and supporting undo. Fix costly errors first.
- Minimalism does not mean flat design.

**Heuristic Evaluation** (video, Nielsen, 2019). https://www.nngroup.com/videos/heuristic-evaluation/
- Reviewers judge a UI against the 10 principles. The page does not cover process or severity detail.

**Visual Hierarchy: Definition** (Gordon, 2021). https://www.nngroup.com/articles/visual-hierarchy-ux-definition/
- Contrast against surroundings creates emphasis, not color per se. Reserve saturated color for key items and warm red for errors.
- Use about 2 primary and 2 secondary colors and 3 or fewer contrast treatments. Emphasizing everything emphasizes nothing.
- Don't rely on color alone. Pair it with size, weight or position.
- Use 3 or fewer type sizes and 2 or fewer large elements. The most important element is the largest.
- Group with proximity and whitespace first. Use borders or backgrounds only when spacing fails. Keep headings close to their own content.
- Squint/blur test. Account for real content, since a strong image can hijack hierarchy. Define the content hierarchy before designing.

**Visual Hierarchy** (video, Gordon, 2022). https://www.nngroup.com/videos/visual-hierarchy/
- Color and size signal importance. Proximity and shared containers signal relationship.

**Findability vs. Discoverability** (video, Harley, 2020). https://www.nngroup.com/videos/findability-vs-discoverability/
- Findability means locating something you are looking for. Discoverability means encountering something you weren't.
- The two need different research methods to evaluate.

**Design Systems 101** (Fessenden, 2021, reviewed 2026). https://www.nngroup.com/articles/design-systems-101/
- The repository has three parts: a style guide (brand, voice, visual and interaction standards), a component library (named components with attributes, states and code) and a pattern library (layouts and templates).
- Benefits: speed, consistency, a shared vocabulary, onboarding, and designers freed for hard problems.
- Costs: a dedicated ongoing team (at minimum interaction design, visual design and development) plus teaching time.
- You can adopt, adapt or build a system. It is not worth it for one-offs or prototypes, and pays off over years of repeated work.

**Design Systems and Their Benefits** (video, Pernice, 2018). https://www.nngroup.com/videos/design-systems/
- A design system is broader than a style guide. It aims for engaging and consistent UI within short dev cycles at low cost.

**Accessibility Is Not Enough** (Nielsen, 2005). https://www.nngroup.com/articles/accessibility-is-not-enough/
- Passing technical checklists does not mean disabled users can complete their tasks.
- Test with disabled users on realistic tasks using their own assistive tech.
- Guidelines like "add alt text" don't say what the text must communicate for the task.
- Disability is a spectrum, and needs overlap with older users and everyone else.
- "Renders correctly" is not the bar. "Navigable and understandable" is.

**Top 10 Enduring Web-Design Mistakes** (Schade et al., 2016). https://www.nngroup.com/articles/top-10-enduring/
- Content placed by org-chart logic, or links with overlapping labels, leaves users unable to choose. Fix with card sorting, tree testing and renaming.
- Islands of information (related content unlinked or duplicated) and repetitive links or long paths. Every click should advance the goal.
- Hidden fees or prices revealed late. Show cost before commitment.
- Microsites strand users with no clear way back.
- Poor search (relevance, scope, snippets) and flawed filters (one-size, either/or, bad tagging).
- Walls of text (write for scanning) and links that look like ads (keep links plain and distinct).

## 3. Consolidated agent-checkable rules

Format for each rule: **check**, then **evidence for PASS / FAIL**, then source. Each rule is tagged by what an agent needs to check it:
- **[S]** static: the screen, screenshot or DOM alone
- **[F]** flow: walking a task end to end
- **[R]** needs real-user research, which an agent can only flag, not pass

### 3A. Nielsen's 10 heuristics as checks
Source for all of 3A: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics/

**H1 Visibility of system status**
- [S] Location: every screen shows where the user is (title, active nav item, breadcrumb, TUI header or path).
  - PASS: the active location is identifiable without interacting.
  - FAIL: no active state, or a generic title.
- [F] Feedback: every user action gets a response within about 0.1s (acknowledge). Every operation over about 1s shows progress, and long ones show determinate progress. These thresholds are general HCI knowledge, not stated in this article.
  - PASS: a spinner, progress bar, streaming tokens or "working…" line is present.
  - FAIL: a silent wait, or a button that doesn't change state on click.
- [S] Consequential state: before a destructive or committing action, the current state is shown (selection count, target environment, branch).
  - FAIL: "Delete" without showing what is selected.

**H2 Match between system and the real world**
- [S] Vocabulary: labels use user-domain words.
  - FAIL: internal IDs, enum names, error codes, implementation nouns ("payload", "entity", "null") in user-facing copy.
  - PASS: every label is understandable to the persona without a glossary.
- [S] Order: information follows the user's natural or task order (chronological, spatial, the order of the physical analog).
  - FAIL: fields ordered by database schema.

**H3 User control and freedom**
- [F] Every non-trivial state has a visible exit: Cancel or Back, Esc in a TUI, a close control on modals.
  - FAIL: a modal with no close, a wizard with no back, a TUI mode with no documented quit.
- [F] Destructive or high-impact actions are undoable, or at minimum confirmable.
  - PASS: undo, a toast with "Undo", or a soft delete.
  - FAIL: irreversible action on a single click.

**H4 Consistency and standards**
- [S] Internal consistency: the same action has the same label, icon, position and style everywhere. Same word, same meaning.
  - FAIL: "Remove", "Delete" and "Trash" used for one action. Primary button on the right on one screen and the left on another.
- [S] External consistency (Jakob's Law): platform conventions are followed. Examples: link styling, logo goes home, Cmd/Ctrl-shortcuts, `--help`/`-h` in CLIs, `q`/Esc in TUIs.
  - FAIL: a convention is broken without a documented reason.
- [S] Design-system conformance: components come from the system's library, and their states match the spec.
  - FAIL: one-off components that duplicate system components. Source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/design-systems-101/

**H5 Error prevention**
- [S] Constraints: inputs constrain format (pickers, masks, enums), defaults are sensible, and invalid actions are disabled with the reason shown.
  - FAIL: free-text where a picker fits. A disabled button with no explanation.
- [F] Commitments: high-cost actions require confirmation that names the consequence ("Delete 14 files from prod?"), not a generic "Are you sure?".
- [S] Prioritize: errors with the highest cost are addressed first in any finding list.

**H6 Recognition rather than recall**
- [F] No cross-screen memory: the user never has to remember a value from screen A to type into screen B.
  - FAIL: "note this code, then go to settings…"
- [S] Options are visible or discoverable. Commands are listed (menus, command palette, TUI key-hint footer). Recent and suggested items are offered.
  - FAIL: a hidden-gesture-only or memorized-command-only path for a core task.

**H7 Flexibility and efficiency of use**
- [S] Accelerators exist for frequent actions (keyboard shortcuts, CLI flags, saved views) and **do not intrude on novices**.
  - PASS: shortcuts shown as hints and the novice path still complete.
  - FAIL: a core task that only works via a shortcut.

**H8 Aesthetic and minimalist design**
- [S] Each element earns its place: it either communicates or affords action. Slogans #3/#4: https://www.nngroup.com/videos/communication-not-decoration-ux-slogan-4/
  - FAIL: decorative elements competing with the primary task, or rarely-needed info at equal prominence to the primary content.
- Note: minimalism does not mean flat. Rich styling passes if it supports communication.

**H9 Help users recognize, diagnose, recover from errors**
- [S] Every error message has three parts: plain-language **what** happened, **why** or where (the precise field or cause), and **how to fix** (an action or button).
  - FAIL: raw codes, stack traces or "Something went wrong" alone.
  - PASS: all three parts present, plus visually prominent styling placed near the cause. Error styling must not rely on color alone; see V4.

**H10 Help and documentation**
- [S] Help is contextual (inline hints, tooltips, `--help` per subcommand), searchable, task-oriented and stepwise.
  - FAIL: help exists only as a separate long manual. Instructions given as prose paragraphs instead of numbered steps.

### 3B. Visual-design and hierarchy checks
Sources: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/principles-visual-design/ and https://www.nngroup.com/articles/visual-hierarchy-ux-definition/

- **V1 Squint test [S]:** blur the screenshot (for example, a Gaussian blur of about 8px) and identify the first focal point.
  - PASS: it is the screen's primary task element.
  - FAIL: no clear focal point, or the focus lands on decoration or secondary content.
- **V2 Type scale [S]:** count distinct font sizes in one view.
  - PASS: about 3 tiers (small, medium, large).
  - FAIL: more than about 4 competing sizes, or more than 2 "large" elements.
- **V3 Color budget [S]:** count accent or saturated colors used for emphasis.
  - PASS: about 2 primary plus 2 secondary, and 3 or fewer contrast treatments.
  - FAIL: everything emphasized.
- **V4 Color not sole signal [S]:** every color-coded meaning (error, status, category) also has a non-color cue (icon, text, weight, position).
- **V5 Text contrast [S]:** run a contrast check on all text. De-emphasis must use size, weight or position, not low contrast. WCAG thresholds (4.5:1 body, 3:1 large text) are the standard external reference; NN/g only says to use a checker.
  - FAIL: grey-on-grey "secondary" text below threshold.
- **V6 Proximity [S]:** each label sits closer to its own control than to any other. Headings sit closer to their own section than to the previous one. Groups are separated by more space than items within a group.
- **V7 Containers last [S]:** borders and cards are used only where spacing can't do the grouping.
  - FAIL: nested boxes-in-boxes.
- **V8 Red reserved [S]:** warm red is used only for errors, warnings and destructive actions.
- **V9 Real-content stress [S]:** render with realistic, longest and empty content, and check hierarchy still holds. A strong image or long name must not hijack it.

### 3C. Interaction-cost and content checks

- **C1 Step count [F]:** count the clicks, keystrokes, screens and fields for the primary task. Compare alternatives. Every step must advance the goal.
  - FAIL: repeated choices or detours. Sources: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/interaction-cost-definition/ and https://www.nngroup.com/articles/top-10-enduring/
- **C2 Choice count [S]:** count decisions demanded per screen. Flag screens with options that serve no key task. Slogans #2, #9, #12: https://www.nngroup.com/videos/more-choices-more-trouble-ux-slogan/
- **C3 Scannability [S]:** text blocks have headings, short paragraphs, bullets and front-loaded keywords. Fail on walls of text. Sources: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/top-10-enduring/ and https://www.nngroup.com/videos/brevity-brilliance-ux-slogan-5/
- **C4 Target size [S]:** interactive targets are large and well-spaced. 44×44pt / 24×24 CSS px are platform or WCAG references, not NN/g numbers. Source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/interaction-cost-definition/
- **C5 Label distinctness [S]:** no two sibling nav or menu labels plausibly lead to the same place. Source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/top-10-enduring/
- **C6 Cost before commitment [F]:** prices, fees, time and irreversibility are shown before the user starts a flow. Source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/top-10-enduring/
- **C7 Way back [F]:** every sub-area, microsite or modal has a visible route to its parent. Source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/top-10-enduring/
- **C8 Links look like links [S]:** interactive text is visually distinct and doesn't resemble ads or decoration. Source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/top-10-enduring/
- **C9 IA before nav [R/S]:** an IA inventory (content list plus relationships) exists before navigation is chosen, and the navigation pattern fits the hierarchy's depth. Source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ia-vs-navigation/

### 3D. Process checks: what the agent must flag rather than claim

- **P1 Evidence label:** every design claim is tagged *observed* (user data), *heuristic* (rule-based) or *opinion*. A heuristic pass is never reported as "usable". Sources: https://www.nngroup.com/videos/you-are-not-user-slogan/ and https://www.nngroup.com/videos/checking-guessing-ux-slogan/
- **P2 Problem stated first:** each proposal names the user problem and its evidence before the solution. FAIL: a solution with no problem statement. Source: https://www.nngroup.com/videos/stop-solutioneering/
- **P3 Accessibility ≠ checklist:** an automated a11y pass is reported as "technically conformant", never as "accessible". The task-completion question stays open until tested. Source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/accessibility-is-not-enough/
- **P4 Research-method fit:** when recommending research, state attitudinal or behavioral, qualitative or quantitative, and generative, formative or summative. Use about 5 users per qualitative round per segment and about 20–35 or more for quantitative. Sources: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/which-ux-research-methods/ and https://www.nngroup.com/articles/why-you-only-need-to-test-with-5-users/
- **P5 Persona discipline:** any persona detail must map to a design decision, and personas must cite research, never be invented. Source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/persona/

## 4. Durable vs. NN/g opinion

### Durable: grounded in perception and cognition research, or stable across decades
- The Gestalt principles, especially proximity. These come from early-20th-century perception psychology. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/principles-visual-design/
- Recognition beats recall (a memory-research finding), and memory load is a cost. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics/
- Mental models drive behavior and are sticky. This is Norman and the cognitive tradition. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mental-models/
- Behavior and self-report diverge. This is robust across social science. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/which-ux-research-methods/
- Interaction cost as a summable effort is GOMS-like and well grounded. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/interaction-cost-definition/
- The 10 heuristics as categories. They derive from factor analysis and have been broadly validated for 30 years. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics/
- The slip/mistake distinction (Norman/Reason).
- Contrast for legibility, and color not being a sole signal (color-vision deficiency is physiological).

### NN/g opinion, house style, or empirically contested
- **The "5 users" number.** The formula assumes a uniform per-user discovery rate L≈31%. Later research (e.g. Faulkner 2003; Spool & Schroeder 2001) showed wide variance by product and task. Treat it as "small iterative rounds", not "85% guaranteed". NN/g itself adds caveats. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/why-you-only-need-to-test-with-5-users/
- **The "10% of budget doubles quality" figure** is NN/g's own internal metric. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-101-introduction-to-usability/
- **Numeric visual budgets** (3 or fewer sizes, 2+2 colors, 3 or fewer contrast treatments, inverted-L ≈ 4 levels) are pedagogical heuristics, not research findings. Expressive or editorial design legitimately breaks them. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/visual-hierarchy-ux-definition/
- **"You can't impose joy" and "communication not decoration"** are strong functionalist positions. They undervalue brand, aesthetic-usability effects and emotional design (which Norman himself wrote about). Use them as a check against decoration that blocks the task, not as a ban on character. https://www.nngroup.com/videos/you-cant-impose-joy-ux-slogan-3/
- **"Too easy is impossible"** holds for task UI, but not for games, learning products or deliberate friction (safety confirmations, which H5 itself endorses). https://www.nngroup.com/videos/too-easy/
- **The six-phase design-thinking model** is one of several framings (Stanford d.school, IDEO, the Double Diamond). Its phase names are convention. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/design-thinking/
- **"Why beats what"** is a value priority. Analytics-led teams would dispute it. https://www.nngroup.com/videos/why-beats-what-ux-slogan-6/
- **Age.** Several core items are old: Definition of UX (1998), 5 Users (2000), Accessibility (2005), IA vs. Nav (2014). They are still reviewed, but their examples are web-centric and predate AI interfaces.

## 6. What this changes for Luna (design director) and Mika (design engineer)

### Luna (direction, options, decision record)
1. **Problem before options.** Every Atelier board opens with a problem statement and its evidence class (observed, heuristic or assumed) before showing options. Slogans #18 and #11. https://www.nngroup.com/videos/stop-solutioneering/
2. **The decision record tags evidence.** Each pick records *why* with an evidence label. "Luna prefers it" is logged as opinion, never as validation. Slogans #1, #13, #15. https://www.nngroup.com/videos/show-me-the-data/
3. **Name the duality.** When options trade off (density vs. clarity, novelty vs. convention), Luna states which pole each option takes and why this context wants it, instead of presenting a "best" option. "It Depends" and "Dualities". https://www.nngroup.com/videos/dualities-user-experience/
4. **Write product design principles.** Codify 3–7 trade-off value statements per product (e.g. "status over silence") as the top layer of the system. They arbitrate future boards. https://www.nngroup.com/videos/design-principles-101/
5. **Convention is the default and novelty must be justified.** An option that breaks a platform or Jakob's Law convention carries an explicit justification and a mental-model risk note. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mental-models/
6. **Content hierarchy precedes layout.** Before visual options, list the screen's content in priority order (key takeaway first), and do IA before navigation. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/visual-hierarchy-ux-definition/ and https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ia-vs-navigation/
7. **Recommend the next research step.** Each session ends with the cheapest study that would falsify the chosen direction, sized per P4 (e.g. 5-user think-aloud on the primary task). Luna cannot claim usability. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-research-cheat-sheet/
8. **Decoration audit.** Luna keeps character and brand, but any decorative layer must pass "does it communicate or support the task". This counters "you can't impose joy" and doesn't ban delight; see section 4. https://www.nngroup.com/videos/communication-not-decoration-ux-slogan-4/

### Mika (vetting, codifying, enforcing the system)
1. **Run 3A–3C as a lint pass on every vetted option.** Static checks (V1–V9, H2, H4, H8, H9) run on a screenshot or DOM. Flow checks (H1, H3, H5, H6, C1, C6, C7) run by scripting the primary task. Output is pass/fail plus evidence (a crop or the DOM node).
2. **Automated measurements:** squint-blur focal point, font-size tier count, accent-color count, contrast ratios, label-to-field proximity deltas, step and keystroke counts, and choice count per screen.
3. **Codify the system NN/g's way:** a style guide (tokens, voice), a component library (named components with full **state** coverage: default, hover, focus, disabled-with-reason, loading, error, empty) and a pattern library (templates). Mika flags one-off components as H4 failures. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/design-systems-101/
4. **Bake error prevention and recovery into components.** Destructive buttons require a consequence-naming confirmation or undo. The error component structurally requires what/why/fix slots, so a bare "Error" can't be rendered. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics/
5. **Accessibility honesty.** Mika reports axe/WCAG passes as "technically conformant" and keeps a standing open item for assistive-tech task testing. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/accessibility-is-not-enough/
6. **Stress real content.** Every vetted option renders with long, empty, error and many-items data before sign-off (V9).
7. **Fidelity choice is explicit.** Mika states which fidelity axes (interactivity, visual, content) a prototype has and what question it can and cannot answer, and pilot-runs it before any user sees it. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-prototype-hi-lo-fidelity/
8. **TUI and agent-surface translation.** For Paradigm and Gnomon's TUI and agent surfaces, Mika applies the section 5 mapping. Examples: status line equals H1, Esc/Ctrl-C equals H3, key-hint footer equals H6, and in a terminal color is never the sole signal.

### The "eye" upgrade in one line
Have the agents see a screen the way NN/g's heuristic evaluator does: hierarchy, cost, convention, status, recovery. Have them label every judgment by its evidence. And have them always hand off the question only real users can answer.
